Monthly and fixed deposits

A monthly deposit, often called a recurring deposit, is a fixed amount you pay in every month for a set term. A fixed deposit is one lump sum left untouched for the term. Both pay interest, and both pay it out with your money at the end.

Put $200 a month into a five-year deposit at 6%, with interest added every three months, and you pay in $12,000. At maturity you get $14,012.94, so $2,012.94 is interest. A $10,000 fixed deposit at 5.5% for three years, added quarterly, grows to $11,780.68.

Why “interest added” matters

When interest is added to the balance, the next interest is worked out on the bigger amount. Adding it monthly or quarterly earns a little more than adding it once a year, and much more than paying it only at maturity on a long deposit. Your bank’s terms say which one it uses. Match it in Interest added and the page follows it month by month.

After tax and charges

In some countries the bank takes tax from the interest before paying it. In others you declare it with your income. If yours is taken at source, type the rate, and add any yearly account charge too. The page then shows what actually reaches you, and the yearly return that works out to.

Questions

They suit different situations. A fixed deposit earns more on a lump sum you already have, because all of it earns interest from day one. A recurring deposit suits saving from your monthly pay.

Banks round interest at each step, count days differently, or add interest on specific dates. The page’s figure should be close; your bank’s statement is the final word.
