    How a rotating savings group works

    A few people agree to pay the same amount every month. Each month the whole pot goes to one of them, and the group keeps going until everyone has had a turn. Six people paying $200 a month means a $1,200 pot every month for six months.

    It goes by many names: a committee in Pakistan and India, a chit in South India, a tanda in Mexico, a susu in West Africa and the Caribbean, a pardner in Jamaica and the UK, a hui among Chinese families, a somiti in Bangladesh. Economists call it a ROSCA, a rotating savings and credit association.

    Who comes out ahead?

    Nobody gets more money than they put in. What changes is when they get it. In the example, Amara gets $1,200 in January after paying only $200, so for her the group is a $1,000 loan with no interest, paid back over five months. Farid gets his $1,200 in June after paying in all of it, so for him it was a way to save.

    That’s why the order matters, and why groups usually agree on it out loud: by drawing names, by who needs it first, or by putting the most trusted people early.

    Savings fund

    Some groups don’t hand out the pot. They keep it together, often in a bank, and share it at the end. Eight people saving $100 a month for two years pay in $19,200. At a steady 6% a year, added monthly, the fund would reach about $20,447.29, so each person’s share is $2,555.91.

    Rules worth agreeing before the first payment

    • The payment day. And what happens when someone is late.
    • The order. Write it down and share it in the group chat. The copy button gives you a ready list.
    • What if someone stops paying after their turn? It’s the biggest risk in any rotating group, so talk about it at the start.
    • Who keeps the record. One shared record stops arguments about who paid what.
